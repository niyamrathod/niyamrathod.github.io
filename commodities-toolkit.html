What this taught me about markets

Building this made the physical side click: a commodity's price is really about whether the actual barrel or cargo can get to where it's needed. Storage tells you if the market is tight or drowning, chokepoints decide whether supply can even move, and the shape of the forward curve is the market saying which of those it believes. A full tank or a blocked strait moves things more than a scary headline that fades.

Why I built this

After my BP internship modelling crude and gas supply-demand, I wanted one screen that put the whole physical picture in one place, the ships, the storage, and the curve, instead of three tabs that never talked to each other. The map is the way in; the fundamentals and the curve are the substance behind it.

What I'd still improve

The vessels and prices here are illustrative so the page runs offline. The live version plugs into AISStream for real ship positions and the EIA and European gas-storage APIs for the fundamentals, refreshed on the same GitHub Action that runs my morning brief. Cargo contents and freight rates are limited on free data, so the map speaks in vessel types, not what's inside the hull.

How systematic traders read a commodity price

Trend, momentum, and mean reversion are the three tools most systematic commodity funds start with. None of them predict the future — they read the recent past in a disciplined, repeatable way. Work through each lesson to understand the logic behind the signal, then use the experiments to build intuition for how the parameters change the output.

Lesson 1 of 4
Trend following — the moving-average crossover
A moving-average crossover does not predict the future. It confirms that a trend is already happening. When the short-term average crosses above the long-term average, a trend-follower goes long — not because the price must keep rising, but because the evidence says it already has been. CTAs and managed-futures funds run exactly this signal 24/7 across 50+ markets. The edge is not in any individual trade. It is in applying the rule consistently across many uncorrelated assets over years.
💡 The signal lags by design. A 20/100 crossover misses the first 5% of a move. That cost is worth it to avoid reacting to the noise visible in the middle of the chart.
The 20-day SMA just crossed below the 100-day SMA. What does the trend-following signal say?
Experiment: drag the windows
Set the fast SMA to 5 days and watch the green/red shading flicker through the choppy middle section. That rapid switching is called whipsawing — being stopped in and out for small losses each time. Longer windows give cleaner signals but miss the start and end of every trend.
Lesson 2 of 4
Time-series momentum — the trailing return
Momentum asks a simpler question: is this market higher than it was N months ago? If yes, go long. The "12-1" label from equity research means a 12-month lookback skipping the most recent month to avoid microstructure noise. In commodities, traders often use shorter windows because supply shocks resolve faster than in equities.

Unlike the crossover, momentum produces a single number: the total return over the lookback window. Positive = long. Negative = short. Nothing more.
💡 Momentum and trend-following often agree — but not always. A slow SMA crossover can stay long even when the trailing 4-month return is negative. They are measuring different things.
The 120-day return for Brent crude is −11%. What does the momentum signal say?
Experiment: change the lookback period
Shorten to 40 days and the signal catches the price shock earlier but also flips more during the choppy period. Extend to 250 days and the signal barely moves — it is carrying information from almost a year ago, so today's shock barely registers.
Lesson 3 of 4
Mean reversion — when to fade the move
Mean reversion is the opposite bet to trend-following. It says: prices that move far from their recent average tend to snap back. A z-score above +2 means the price is more than 2 standard deviations above its rolling mean — statistically unusual enough to fade.

Commodities can sustain genuine trends when supply is structurally short, but they also over-react to news. Mean reversion captures the snap-back after the overshoot. The key question every time: is this a real structural move, or just noise that will reverse?
⚠️ During the price shock on this chart, trend says short and mean reversion says long. The conflict is real — which signal you follow depends on whether you believe the shock is structural or temporary.
The z-score just hit +2.6. What does the mean-reversion signal say?
Experiment: change the sensitivity
Narrow the band to ±1 and the signal fires almost constantly — too much noise. Widen to ±3 and it almost never fires. The ±2 default balances signal frequency against statistical significance.
Lesson 4 of 4
Why carry changes everything in commodities
In equities, you either ride a signal or you do not. In commodities, the forward curve gives you a third source of return: the roll yield.

When the curve is in backwardation — front month priced above back months — a long futures position earns a positive roll yield as the near contract expires and rolls into the cheaper far one. Contango does the reverse: it charges you to hold a long. This asymmetry is unique to commodity futures. Backwardation does not just help a long — it pays you even if the price goes nowhere.

Summary
When each signal works — and when it fails
1Trend following
Works when supply shocks create sustained directional moves: OPEC cuts, production outages, demand surges that take months to resolve.
Fails when price oscillates without conviction, generating repeated whipsaws and small losses on each reversal.
2Momentum
Works when a trend is mature and being reinforced by physical flows and fund positioning all moving the same direction.
Fails when the lookback window straddles a regime change, carrying stale bullish or bearish information into the new environment.
3Mean reversion
Works when a shock gets over-priced and the physical market adjusts, pulling price back toward equilibrium.
Fails when a structural shift makes the "stretched" level the new normal. Selling a real OPEC cut as noise is expensive.
+Roll yield (carry)
Backwardation: paid to hold a long even if price is flat. Amplifies any long signal — the market is tight, storage is low.
Contango: charged to hold a long. Acts as a constant drag on every long position regardless of the price signal.
The through-line. Fundamentals and storage set how tight the market is, that tightness shows up as the shape of the forward curve, and the map is where you see whether the physical barrels can actually move to relieve it. Same chain a desk reasons along, in one screen.
